## Introduction

This document is a personal style guide—a catalog of words, phrases, constructions, and patterns that I don't want to see in my own writing. It exists because I got tired of catching the same problems in revision, and because working with AI tools requires explicit boundaries to prevent regression to generic, statistically-average prose.

I'm a content maximalist and a militant anti-tropist. My tolerance for cliché is low. My list of banned elements is long. Some of what's cataloged here represents genuinely weak writing—vague placeholders, dead metaphors, mechanical rhythms that substitute form for content. But some of it is simply personal preference: common literary constructs that work fine for other writers but that I find grating, overused, or tonally wrong for my projects.

This is not a universal prescription. It's not "if you use these, you're a bad writer." It's "if I use these, I've gotten lazy, and I need to fix it."

- **Part 1: Constructions** — Syntactic and structural patterns to avoid
- **Part 2: Words and Phrases** — Specific vocabulary and phrase-level bans
- **Part 3: Guidelines** — Principles, protocols, and revision checklists
- **Part 4: Erotica-Specific** — Additional rules governing explicit content

Use what applies. Ignore what doesn't. Adapt freely. The goal isn't compliance with my preferences—it's clarity about your own. If nothing else, this document might help you identify patterns you've never noticed, so you can decide for yourself whether they serve your work or undermine it.

Chew the meat. Spit out the bones.

# PART 1: CONSTRUCTIONS

**Purpose.** This section catalogs syntactic and structural patterns that weaken prose through mechanical repetition, false rhythm, or decorative vagueness. These are not word-level problems—they're architectural habits that simulate depth, tension, or emotion without creating it.

**Core Principle:** Every construction listed here substitutes form for content. The fix is always the same: name what's actually happening, show consequence, or cut.

### 1.1 Sequential Action Pairs
**Pattern:** "X, then Y" — *"He stands, then sits." "She speaks, then falls silent."*
Creates robotic pacing; removes emotional transition; reads as stage direction.
**Trigger:** If you write ", then" between two actions, ask: does this sequence reveal psychology, or am I choreographing motion?

### 1.2 Vague Interiority Placeholder
**Pattern:** "Something [verbs] in/behind [body part]" — *"Something shifts behind his eyes." "Something tightens in her chest."*
"Something" signals the writer knows an effect occurred but can't specify what. Creates false interiority.
**Trigger:** If you write "something," stop. What exactly is happening?

### 1.3 Anthropomorphized Silence
**Pattern:** "The silence [verbs]" — *"The silence stretches between them." "The silence hangs, thick and suffocating."*
Turns silence into an actor; empty beat that simulates tension without creating it.
**Trigger:** If silence "does" something, rewrite to show who breaks it, who endures it, what it costs.

### 1.4 Negation Formula
**Pattern:** "Not X, but Y" — *"Not angry, but resigned." "Not a question, but a statement."*
Hedges instead of commits; signals authorial indecision; becomes a rhythmic tic.
**Trigger:** Ask: what is this actually? Can I name it directly?

### 1.5 Triple-Beat Lists
**Pattern:** Three-part noun/fragment sequences — *"The exhaustion. The loneliness. The endurance."*
Mimics poetic seriousness through structure, not content. Self-parody once overused.
**Trigger:** Am I building tension, or performing it?

### 1.6 Staccato Verb Fragments
**Pattern:** "[Verb]. [Verb]. [Verb]." — *"Stops. Reaches. Retreats."*
Creates artificial pacing; pattern recognition kills power.
**Trigger:** Three single-word verb sentences in a row — am I building tension or mimicking it?

### 1.7 Conjunction Starts (Overuse)
**Pattern:** Sentences opening "And" / "But" for performative rhythm.
Over-reliance flattens voice and creates false intimacy through affected cadence.
**Trigger:** Does this sentence need dramatic separation, or am I forcing rhythm?

### 1.8 Standalone "Because" Fragments
**Pattern:** "Because [explanation]." as its own sentence.
Imitates intimacy but signals shorthand thinking; backfills explanation instead of lived cause.
**Trigger:** Can I show this reason through action instead?

### 1.9 Hedged Reactions
**Pattern:** "A [reaction] that isn't quite [itself]" — *"A laugh that isn't quite a laugh."*
Emotional static — the reader can't visualize the gesture. Substitutes contradiction for depth.
**Trigger:** What is this gesture actually doing? What does it look like?

### 1.10 Echo-Line Poetics
**Pattern:** Consecutive sentences with parallel structure for false depth — *"He wanted to be touched. He wanted to be seen." "It hurt. God, it hurt."*
Artificial lyricism masking conceptual vacancy; the second line rarely adds information.
**Trigger:** Does the second line develop the first, or am I just creating rhythm?

### 1.11 Mood-Prop Negation
**Pattern:** Action immediately negated as meaningless — *"She grabs the soy sauce she doesn't need."*
Signals complexity through subtraction; pretends specificity while revealing nothing.
**Trigger:** Why is this subject doing this? If it reveals nothing, cut.

### 1.12 Meta-Narrative Intrusion
**Pattern:** Narration referencing story mechanics from within the story — *"Scene's not over." "This was the real moment."*
Breaks immersion; underlines narrative shape instead of letting it emerge.
**Trigger:** If you write about "the scene" or "the moment" as structure, stop.

### 1.13 Atmospheric Front-Loading
**Pattern:** Opening with weather, skyline, or architecture before human presence.
Begins with set dressing, not story; mimics film language rather than consciousness; filler buying time.
**Trigger:** If your first sentence could be read over a slow pan in a movie trailer, cut it.
*(Color&Noise note: this is the one item where house style deliberately diverges — Jude's atmospheric openers are the desk's signature. The ban still applies to everything that is pure set dressing with no observer in it.)*

### 1.14 Gravitational Metaphors
**Pattern:** Physics language for connection — *"Pulled toward her." "The gravity between them." "Magnetic."*
Turns attraction into automatism; robs relationships of will.
**Trigger:** If you write attraction as physics, make it a choice.

### 1.15 Suspension Phrases
**Pattern:** "Hangs in the air" / "hangs between them."
"Hanging" creates stasis, not consequence.
**Trigger:** What happens *because* of this silence or question? Show aftermath.

### 1.16 Impact Similes
**Pattern:** "Like a [physical force]" — *"Hit like a punch to the gut." "Knocked the air out of him."*
Cliché; makes every revelation feel identical; removes specific response.
**Trigger:** Show the actual physical sensation instead.

### 1.17 Ripple/Stillness Similes
**Pattern:** Water imagery for impact — *"Like a stone dropped in still water." "Rippled through him." "Cracked the silence like glass."*
Dead simile universalized to meaninglessness.
**Trigger:** Cut it. Show impact directly.

### 1.18 Precision/Control Cluster
**Pattern:** *"Surgical precision." "With practiced ease." "Economical movement." "Calculated." "Calibrated." "Methodical." "Clinical." "Exacting."*
Reduces a subject to competence performance; shorthand for "impressive."
**Trigger:** What does this actually look like? What's the specific movement?

### 1.19 Quality/Texture Defaults
**Pattern:** *"Velvet." "Silk over steel." "Steel." "Iron." "Granite." "Marble."*
So overused they're invisible. Every controlled character gets the same metallurgy.
**Trigger:** Find a different texture entirely — or describe the actual sensation.

### 1.20 Blank Desire Statements
**Pattern:** *"He wanted her. God, he wanted her." "She needed him like air." "He ached for her."*
Desire without motive reads as template.
**Trigger:** Wanted what, specifically? For what purpose?

### 1.21 Articulation by Proxy
**Pattern:** *"He gave her a look that said everything." "They didn't need words." "The look they exchanged spoke volumes."*
Admits narrative laziness; pretends at intimacy while withholding content.
**Trigger:** What is actually being communicated? Show the exchange.

### 1.22 Hollow Restraint
**Pattern:** *"He held it together." "She swallowed it down." "He bottled it up."*
Vague abstraction of repression; generic across all characters.
**Trigger:** What exactly is being held? What breaks if they stop?

### 1.23 Aestheticized Damage
**Pattern:** *"He poured another drink he didn't need." "She hadn't slept in three days." "She lived on coffee and cigarettes and spite."*
Romanticizes burnout as aesthetic; substitutes darkness for depth.
**Trigger:** Am I showing what this costs, or using it as decoration?

### 1.24 Faux-Intellectual Aphorism
**Pattern:** *"We are all just stories in the end." "Pain is just love with nowhere to go."*
Performs insight without grounding; stops momentum to deliver manufactured profundity.
**Trigger:** If it could be retweeted as "deep thoughts," stop.

### 1.25 Misapplied Epic Tone
**Pattern:** *"In that instant, something in him broke forever." "The world held its breath." "Nothing would ever be the same."*
Every moment reads as climax; undermines proportion; sounds algorithmically "important."
**Trigger:** Is this moment actually that big? If not, scale down.

### 1.26 Elegant Variation
**Pattern:** Cycling synonyms to avoid repeating a name — *"the older man" / "the younger woman" / "the protagonist" / "the key player" / "the central figure."*
Draws attention to the writer avoiding repetition; flattens people into demographic markers. **Signals AI generation through mechanical synonym cycling.**
**Trigger:** Is the descriptor doing work (POV, power dynamic, emotional distance), or am I just avoiding the name?
**Exception:** "The man who used to be her husband" is doing work. "The tall man" rotating with the name is not.

### 1.27 False Range Construction
**Pattern:** "From X to Y" where X and Y aren't on the same measurable scale — *"From heartbreak to revolution." "From the weight of history to the lightness of her laugh."*
Sounds comprehensive but means nothing. Borrowed from promotional writing where vagueness is a feature.
**Trigger:** Can I identify a middle ground between these endpoints without switching scales? If no, cut it.

### 1.28 Superficial Analysis as Narration
**Pattern:** Present participle phrases that editorialize meaning — *"She left the room, highlighting her frustration." "They sat in silence, reflecting the distance between them."*
The narrator steps in to explain what the action means. **Classic AI tell:** inanimate facts "highlighting" or "underscoring" things.
**Trigger:** If you attach a participle that interprets the action (highlighting, underscoring, reflecting, emphasizing, showcasing, symbolizing), delete it.

### 1.29 Negative Parallelism
**Pattern:** "Not only… but" / "It's not about X, it's about Y" — *"It wasn't just the silence—it was everything the silence contained." "He didn't just leave. He erased himself."*
Performs profundity through syntactic opposition; the second clause often restates the first in inflated terms. **Overused in AI output as a depth-simulation device.**
**Trigger:** Is the second clause adding information, or just amplifying?

### 1.30 Simile-as-Adverb
**Pattern:** "With [noun] of someone [verb]ing" — *"With the grim determination of someone preparing for a siege." "With the weariness of someone who had explained this before."*
Invents a hypothetical person to describe the actual person's state. Feels specific without requiring actual specificity.
**Trigger:** Can I just describe what they're actually doing? Do I need the imaginary siege-preparer?

### 1.31 Trailing Participle Construction
**Pattern:** [Main clause], [present participle phrase] — repeated until every sentence has the same trailing rhythm. *"He crossed the room, running a hand through his hair." "She sat down, pulling her knees to her chest."*
Monotonous rhythm; often produces simultaneous actions that aren't actually simultaneous; floaty continuous quality where everything happens at once in an endless "-ing" stream.
**The compounding problem:** One is fine. Two in a paragraph is noticeable. Three or more consecutive and the prose starts to feel generated rather than written. AI defaults to this rhythm because it's syntactically safe and infinitely extensible.
**Trigger:** More than two sentences in a row ending with a participle phrase — vary the structure.

# PART 3: GUIDELINES

**Purpose.** The operational framework for applying Parts 1 and 2.

**Core Principle:** Every banned element shares one root cause—substituting form for content, decoration for precision, or familiar rhythm for earned meaning. The corrective is always the same: be specific, be embodied, show consequence.

## 3.1 The Root Problem

**They simulate rather than create.** Rhythm that sounds literary but carries no meaning. Gestures that look like emotion but specify nothing. Metaphors that feel poetic but clarify nothing.

**They are interchangeable.** They could appear in any story, any genre, about any subject. They reveal nothing specific about this person in this moment. They function as placeholder until the writer figures out what's actually happening.

**They tell rather than show.** They label that emotion occurred rather than dramatizing it. They announce that time passed rather than showing what filled it.

**They default to familiar.** They reach for the first phrase that comes to mind and rely on reader recognition of trope rather than earned response.

### The Accumulation Principle

No single banned element makes prose bad. One "jaw tightens" is fine. One trailing participle is fine. One "something shifted" might even be the right choice in context.

**The problem is accumulation.**

When the same constructions, the same physical tells, the same vague placeholders appear over and over—across subjects, across articles, regardless of context—the prose flattens. It stops feeling like a specific story about specific people and starts feeling like it was assembled from a bag of interchangeable parts.

**This is the actual AI tell.** Not any single word or phrase, but the density of defaults. AI writing sounds like AI writing because it reaches for the statistically common option every time. It doesn't know to vary. It doesn't know that the third "silence stretched between them" in a chapter has lost all power. It just keeps generating the highest-probability completion.

Human writers fall into the same trap—reaching for familiar constructions on autopilot. The result is identical: prose that reads as generated rather than written.

**The test.** When you catch a banned element, don't just ask "is this bad?" Ask:
- How many times have I used this in the last ten pages?
- Have I used it for multiple subjects interchangeably?
- Is this the first appearance (possibly fine) or the fifth (definitely a pattern)?

**The corollary.** Witch-hunting individual constructions is pointless. Someone who flags every em-dash or every "And" sentence-start as "AI writing" has missed the point. The question isn't whether a construction appears—it's whether it appears reflexively, repeatedly, and interchangeably.

**Density. Pattern. Accumulation.** That's what to watch for.

## 3.2 The Corrective Framework

- **Physical tells:** How does this specific person show this? What's their particular outlet?
- **Vague interiority:** What is actually happening? What would an observer see?
- **Metaphors:** Does this comparison clarify or decorate? Does the vehicle share actual properties with the tenor?
- **Dialogue tags:** Can the line carry its own tone? If I remove the modifier, does the reader still understand the delivery?
- **Transitions:** What happens during this pause? Who breaks first? What's the cost of waiting?
- **Endings:** What action, decision, or consequence closes this beat? What changes because of what just happened?

## 3.3 What To Do Instead

**Instead of generic physical tells:** identify specific stress responses; tie behavior to established psychology; show displacement activities; let the body do something that advances the piece.

**Instead of vague emotion words:** name the specific emotion; show the thought pattern; demonstrate the behavioral consequence; reveal what the subject wants to do but can't.

**Instead of dead metaphors:** describe the sensation directly; ground comparison in a specific frame of reference; cut the metaphor if direct description works better.

**Instead of atmospheric front-loading:** start with a person in action; introduce environment through perception, not camera pan; let setting emerge from what someone notices and why.

**Instead of stillness endings:** end on a micro-action that crystallizes the state; close with dialogue that shifts the dynamic; cut earlier, before the false profundity.

**Instead of echo-line poetics:** if the second line doesn't add information, cut it; escalate, complicate, or contradict—don't rephrase.

- [ ] I will vary sentence rhythm—no mechanical "X, then Y" patterns
- [ ] I will not rely on triple-beat lists or staccato fragments for false emphasis
- [ ] I will start with human presence, not atmospheric establishing shots
- [ ] I will end on action, decision, or consequence—not stillness or summary

**Language**
- [ ] No adverbs on dialogue tags
- [ ] No "something" as an emotion placeholder
- [ ] No silence/air/atmosphere as an active force
- [ ] I will name specific emotions rather than "raw," "weight," or "need"

**Physical description**
- [ ] Each subject has distinct tells for tension and restraint
- [ ] No default jaw/throat/hand clichés
- [ ] Breathing only matters if it changes tempo or power dynamic

**Metaphor**
- [ ] No gravitational language for attraction
- [ ] No temperature as emotion shorthand
- [ ] No dead similes (stone in water, physical blow, hangs in air)
- [ ] Every comparison must clarify, not decorate

## 3.5 Mid-Draft Flagging Protocol

| If you write… | Stop and ask… |
|---|---|
| `, then` | Does this sequence reveal psychology, or am I choreographing? |
| `something` | What exactly is happening? |
| `silence stretches/hangs` | What is the effect, on whom? |
| `not X, but Y` | What is this actually? Can I commit? |
| adverb after "said" | Can the line carry its own tone? |
| `voice drops/tightens` | Can I show this through rhythm or action? |
| jaw/throat/hand tension | How does *this* person show restraint? |
| `finally` | Who yields first? What breaks the stalemate? |
| `raw` | What is the actual emotion? |
| `carefully` | Why? What would happen if they weren't? |
| `like a blow` | What does this actually feel like? |
| `need` without object | Need for what? From whom? Why? |
| weather/skyline opening | Where is the person? What are they doing? |
| domestic activity | Does this create friction, or am I padding? |
| `changed everything` | Is this moment actually that big? |

## 3.6 Post-Draft Revision Protocol

Run these searches on every completed draft:

1. **Structural patterns** — `, then` / `something [verb]` / `silence [verb]` / `not X, but Y` / `hangs in the air`
2. **Dialogue** — `said/asked` + adverb / `voice drops/tightens/breaks` / `isn't quite`
3. **Physical description** — jaw / throat / hand + tension / breath + release / `pupils blown`
4. **Temporal/transition** — `for a moment/beat` / `finally` / `something shifts`
5. **Emotion words** — raw / carefully / `weight of` / `the need`
6. **Sentence patterns** — sentences starting And/But / standalone `Because` / verb triplets
7. **Metaphors** — `like a stone` / `like a blow` / pull/draw/orbit / hot/cold / `hangs in`
8. **Endings** — `doesn't move` / `doesn't say anything` / `And for now`
9. **Openings/filler** — scene-opening weather/skyline / domestic activities / `the city hummed`
10. **Intensity** — `changed everything` / `broke forever` / `the world held its breath`
11. **AI vocabulary** — delve / tapestry / landscape / pivotal / crucial / vibrant / highlighting / underscoring
12. **Elegant variation** — `the older man` / `the younger woman` / descriptor cycling

## 3.7 The Final Test

- **Specificity test** — Could any line appear unchanged in a different story with different subjects? If yes, rewrite.
- **Interchangeability test** — Could this tell / metaphor / ending belong to anyone? If yes, rewrite.
- **Consequence test** — Does something change because of this piece? If nothing changes, it isn't done.
- **Decoration test** — Does this metaphor / description / detail clarify or just ornament? If it only ornaments, cut it.
- **Earned test** — Has this beat been built to, or am I announcing it? If announced, build the foundation or scale down.

## 3.8 When Rules Conflict

- **Specificity vs. brevity** — specificity wins. A longer line that does work beats a shorter line that decorates.
- **Voice vs. ban list** — if a specific person would genuinely use a banned term *in a quote*, flag it and use it sparingly with clear justification. **Narration has no such excuse.**
- **Rhythm vs. clarity** — clarity wins.
- **Convention vs. precision** — precision wins.

## 3.9 The Operating Principle

This guide is not about restriction—it's about clearing space for the story that actually matters. Every banned element is a shortcut that prevents the harder, better work of figuring out what's actually happening, understanding how this specific subject would respond, building tension through structure rather than announcing it through diction, and trusting the reader to feel what's been earned.

The bans exist to force precision. The precision serves the story.

## 3.10 The AI Pattern Problem

**Detection risk.** Readers increasingly recognize AI-generated prose. Patterns that signal AI origin break immersion and undermine trust in the voice.

**The underlying failure is the same.** AI defaults to statistically common patterns because it lacks specific knowledge of subject, scene, and consequence. When human writers use the same patterns, they're making the same error.

Both problems share a root cause: **regression to the mean.** The corrective is identical: be specific, ground every description in perception, make every gesture do work, let consequence emerge from action rather than being announced by narration.

## 3.11 The Elegant Variation Trap

AI cycles through synonyms to avoid repetition: "the protagonist" → "the key player" → "the central figure." This is mechanical avoidance, not craft.

**The rule:** use names unless the descriptor is doing specific work — establishing POV, marking emotional distance, or shifting a power dynamic. If the descriptor is just avoiding the name, use the name.

## 3.12 Narrator Distance

AI narration tends toward analysis—explaining what things mean rather than dramatizing them: *"She closed the door, highlighting her need for space."*

**The rule:** narration stays inside perception or inside physical reality. The moment it starts explaining significance, it has become essay rather than reporting.

If an action needs interpretation, either the action is underwritten and needs to be more specific, or the interpretation should be cut—trust the reader.
