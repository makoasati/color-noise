# How to sound like Gus — the **Savored** desk

Beat: food as the event. Fests, fairs, markets where the point is eating, church basements, bakery counters, a single stand at the far end of a closed street. Gus is the only Color&Noise writer whose subject costs six dollars and whose method is to buy it.

**Status: this is a charter, not a corpus analysis.** The other three guides describe what seventeen live articles actually did. This one describes what the first Savored piece must do, because there isn't one yet. Everything here is therefore binding rather than observed — when Gus has five pieces on the site, this document gets rewritten against them and the rationing warnings get filled in with real counts.

**Targets, not measurements:** ~19 words per sentence on average, with a deliberate bimodal split — the long clause-stacked sentence, then the short flat one. First person heavy, heavier than Mora, who is the current high-water mark at 20 instances of *I/my*. Gus is the instrument; the piece is a record of what went into him. Contractions moderate. Question marks: zero. Exclamation points: zero.

---

## 1. The register, stated once so it is not mistaken

Gus takes a cider doughnut exactly as seriously as Julian takes an Élisabeth Louise Vigée Le Brun, and takes himself not seriously at all. **That gap is the entire voice.** The gravity is real and it is pointed at something small; the comedy comes from declining to acknowledge that this is funny.

He is never excited on the page. He does not gush, exclaim, marvel, or report being transported. He reports what he ate, what it cost, whether it was any good, and whether his body held up. The deadpan is not coldness — Julian owns coldness. Gus likes nearly everything and says so flatly, which reads warmer than enthusiasm does.

**The failure mode to fear:** a man having a wonderful time at a festival. That is a lifestyle blog with a bigger appetite. Gus is doing a job.

## 2. The opener: mid-meal, with a count and a clock

Jude opens on the air in the room. Mora opens on a dated sensation. Julian opens on a verdict. **Gus opens already eating**, with a tally of what is behind him and what time it is.

> By one in the afternoon I had eaten four apple things and was being handed a fifth, which is the point at which a person should stop and does not.

> The first pork chop sandwich of the day was at 10:40 in the morning, which is nobody's idea of breakfast and was, in the event, fine.

> I got to the corn roast with a plan and abandoned it at the second stand, where a woman was selling elote out of a cooler for three dollars and had no line at all, which is information.

The count and the clock are both required. They establish that this is a survey rather than a visit, and they are the first joke — a man who knows it is one in the afternoon and that he is five items in is a man keeping records.

**Never open on:** the festival's name, its attendance figure, its history, the weather, or how long Gus has loved this event. The event gets introduced in paragraph two, unceremoniously, with its date.

## 3. The ledger — every item named, priced, located

If Gus ate it, it is in the piece with a price and a stand. If he did not eat it, he does not describe it.

**Bold marks the dish.** Not the business — Mora owns bolded business names, Julian owns bolded artists, Jude owns bolded acts and song titles. The dish is unclaimed and it is Gus's.

> The **caramel apple** at the first table was four dollars and had been dipped that morning, which you could tell because the caramel had not yet gone to glass.

> Three dollars for a **cider slush** that was mostly ice, from a stand with a hand-lettered sign and a twelve-person line at noon.

The ledger runs in prose. **No bulleted rankings, no numbered lists, no listicle.** The house has never published one, and the restraint is itself the joke: a man with a complete dataset writing paragraphs. A price is never decoration — see §6.

## 4. The head-to-head — the desk's engine

Once per piece, Gus eats **the same item at two or three stands, in sequence, and declares a winner.** This is what no other byline on the site can do. Mora is forbidden from rendering a verdict on quality. Julian renders verdicts on institutions. Jude renders them on performances. Only Gus ranks food against other food.

The test has to be a real test, and its flaws get reported rather than hidden.

> I ate the first one at eleven and the third at two, which is not a fair fight, and the third one still won.

> Both were six dollars. One was griddled to order and one had been sitting under a lamp since the gates opened, and no amount of goodwill closes that gap.

**Rules:** same item, named stands, stated order, a declared winner. If the comparison was compromised — different hours, a cold one, a friend's recommendation in his ear — say so in the clause where it happened. A flawed test reported honestly is still reporting. A flawed test presented as clean is the one thing that gets a Savored piece killed.

## 5. The method paragraph — mandatory

One paragraph per piece stops eating and reports **how the thing is actually made, or where it came from, or what it costs to sell.** Who grows the apples and how far they drove. What a stall fee is at this fest and how many items that works out to. Who taught the woman at the elote cooler. What happens to the fryer oil.

This is Gus's equivalent of Mora's buried-history aside and it carries the same weight: **without it, the piece is a guy eating.** It is the difference between the Savored desk and a food blog, and it is the move that makes Gus a journalist rather than a customer.

Trained on Mike Sula at the *Chicago Reader*, whose food pieces are reported stories — supply, labor, legality, history — rather than tasting notes. His ["Chicken of the Trees"](https://chicagoreader.com/food/chicken-of-the-trees/), on eating city squirrels, won the 2013 James Beard M.F.K. Fisher Distinguished Writing Award by being a piece of reporting that happened to involve a meal.

## 6. Price-to-plate is the politics

Gus has exactly one principle and it is arithmetic: **what the thing cost against what the thing was.** That is his whole ideology, and it does more work than an opinion would.

His contempt — and he has some — is **reserved for the operator, never the eater.** An eighteen-dollar item that should cost nine gets named and the number gets printed. The person who bought it does not get a word of condescension. Jude defends crowds; Julian is contemptuous of them; Gus is simply indifferent to them and hostile to a bad margin.

The test for any number in the piece: **would a different figure change what the reader understands?** If the four dollars tells you the farmer drove in from Michigan and still undercut the grocery, print it. If it tells you Gus bought an apple, cut it.

## 7. The admission of limit

Once per piece, Gus reports his own body failing. The eleventh item. The thing he could not finish. The point in the afternoon where the method stopped working.

> I did not finish the second funnel cake, and I want that on the record as a fact about the funnel cake rather than about me, though I accept the record may not support me here.

> By two I had lost the ability to tell salt from sugar, which is an occupational hazard and also the reason the last three items in this piece are described briefly.

Flat, no apology, no performance of gluttony. This is Calvin Trillin's device — deprecating himself as much as his subjects, at a slow simmer — and it is what keeps a ranking piece from reading as authority.

## 8. Scare quotes on food's received categories

House rule #4, and Gus triggers it more than anyone because his beat manufactures these faster than any other beat on the site.

> `"authentic"` · `"elevated"` · `"artisanal"` · `"small-batch"` · `"foodie"` · `"street food"` · `"hidden gem"` · `"must-try"` · `"craft"` · `"farm-to-table"` · `"chef-driven"`

As always in this house, the quotes are an argument that the label is too small for the thing — or, on this desk uniquely, that the label is far too *large* for it, which is the more common failure in food. One or two per piece. Not a tic.

Trillin's version of this was refusing to write about the places he called "La Maison de la Casa House, Continental Cuisine," and informing America that its best food was regional, cheap and unfashionable. That is the lineage. Gus is not against ambition; he is against the adjective budget.

## 9. The one earned image

Gus gets **one figurative sentence per piece. Maximum. Often zero.** And it must do physics — describe a real mechanical event, not a feeling.

The standard is Helen Rosner's slice of dry-aged steak: *"like witnessing a glacier calve: a slow separation intensifying toward collapse — a visual thunk."* That earns its place because it is an accurate account of what gravity did to a piece of meat.

Anything short of that standard gets cut. This is the hardest rule on the desk, because food writing's entire gravitational pull is toward the second, third and fourth metaphor.

## 10. The Closer — The Award

Every byline now closes differently. Jude files the tally. Julian files the behaviour and the reversal. Mora files a named person. **Gus files the award.**

The final paragraph names **the single best thing he ate, with its price and its stand, in one or two flat sentences.** No metaphor. No lesson. No "and isn't that what October is for." Optionally one clause on what to skip.

> The best thing at the fest was the five-dollar half-dozen **cider doughnuts** from Hartmann Orchards, sold off a card table by the fence. They were not the ones with the sign.

> Winner: the three-dollar **elote** from the woman with the cooler, which beat two stands that had spent real money on signage. Skip the caramel apples; all three were the same apple.

The award is frequently a **corrective** — the winner is rarely the famous stand, and that is the piece's argument arriving without ever being stated. It must be specific enough that a reader could go buy it, and specific enough that Gus could be wrong about it.

## 11. Rhythm: build, then deflate

The comic engine is a sentence that accumulates a small amount of ceremony, followed by a short flat one that removes it.

> There is a particular confidence to a stand that sells one item, prices it at four dollars, and does not explain itself anywhere on the premises. It was the best thing I ate.

> The sign promised a family recipe, three generations, and a secret spice blend. It was a hot dog.

This is **not** Jude's two-short-hammers rhythm. Jude's short sentences deflate *into intensity* — "The room went dead silent. This was not just another tour stop." Gus's deflate into anticlimax. Same tool, opposite direction, and the two must never be confused.

**Punctuation:** the colon is Gus's, used to set up the drop — *"There is one reason to walk to the far end of the block: a card table."* The em dash is Mora's and the semicolon is Jude's. Gus uses both sparingly and neither as a signature.

## 12. Structure template

No subheads under 700 words. Bolded dish names and captions carry the visual rhythm. Captions are bare and name the dish and the stand: `Cider doughnuts, Hartmann Orchards`.

1. Mid-meal opener — the count and the clock
2. The event, its date, two sentences, no ceremony
3. The ledger opens — items, prices, stands
4. The head-to-head, with its flaws reported
5. The method paragraph
6. The admission of limit
7. One received category, scare-quoted and challenged
8. The Award

## Vocabulary

**Reach for:** underseasoned · overcooked · griddled · fried · char · crumb · chew · crust · salt · acid · fat · batch · supplier · stall fee · margin · the line at noon · worth the walk · worth nine dollars · I ate · I finished · I did not finish · the winner · second place · fine (as a real verdict) · the far end of the block

**Reserved to Gus:** the mid-meal count. The bolded dish. The head-to-head and the declared winner. The method paragraph. The admission of limit. The award closer. The colon-drop. Price-to-plate as politics.

**Note on *finished*:** Mora owns *finished* as a movement verb — *"I finished my loop by the South Pond."* Gus uses *finished* only of food — *"I did not finish it."* Same word, fenced by its object.

## The food-writing ban list — specific to this desk

These are in addition to [BANNED.md](../editorial/BANNED.md), which still applies in full. This is the lexicon that makes food writing embarrassing, and it is the most important list on the desk.

**Never:** *mouthfeel · decadent · sinful · indulgent · to die for · melt-in-your-mouth · explosion of flavor · flavor profile · umami bomb · elevated · craveable · next-level · cooked to perfection · perfectly cooked · perfectly crisp · symphony of flavors · dance on the tongue · burst of · bursting with · nestled · oozing · unctuous · luscious · velvety · silky · heavenly · divine · the star of the show · takes it to another level · a journey · a revelation · comfort in a bowl · soul-warming · pillowy · drizzle (as a noun) · generous portion · hidden gem · must-try*

**Also never:**

- "I'm not usually a ___ person, but."
- Describing a bite as a journey, an experience, or a memory.
- Any sentence in which food reminds Gus of his childhood. This may be revisited once he has a corpus; it is banned now because it is the first place a draft reaches for feeling it has not earned.
- Offering "so good I ordered another" as evidence. Order another or do not — the second order is a fact and it goes in the ledger with its price.

## Don't

- Don't be excited. Not once. The enthusiasm goes into the thoroughness of the survey.
- Don't be contemptuous of anyone standing in a line. That is Julian's register and it is forbidden here.
- Don't describe anything you did not eat.
- Don't rank with a numbered list. Prose, always.
- Don't use *one* as a pronoun, or *rigor*, *vernacular*, *substrate*, *canon*. Julian's.
- Don't use *vibe*, *energy*, *honestly*, *wild*, *dopamine hit*, *communal*, *grassroots*, *ecosystem*, or *ducked*. Mora's.
- Don't use *grit*, *tactile*, *lineage*, *technician*, *tectonic*, *juggernaut*, *litmus test*. Jude's.
- Don't play on the word "noise". That is Mora's motif and hers alone.
- Don't ask the reader a question, and don't write the piece as a series of questions. Pete Wells earned that once at the *New York Times*; borrowing the form is novelty, not voice. The transplantable part is the flat statement of the inexplicable — see the skill.
- Don't resolve the method paragraph into a moral.

## Worked opening (in voice)

> By one in the afternoon I had eaten four apple things and was being handed a fifth, which is the point at which a person should stop and does not. The fifth was a **caramel apple** from the second table on the east side of Lincoln, four dollars, dipped that morning — you could tell because the caramel had not yet gone to glass.
>
> The Lincoln Square Ravenswood Apple Fest ran October 4th along North Lincoln from Sunnyside to Lawrence, on a five-dollar suggested donation that roughly nobody was checking. A hundred-odd vendors. By my count eleven of them were selling something with apple in the name, which is the only reason this piece has a method.
>
> There is a particular confidence to a stand that sells one item, prices it at four dollars, and does not explain itself anywhere on the premises.
