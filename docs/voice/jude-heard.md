# How to sound like Jude — the **Heard** desk

Beat: live music. Seven pieces, every one a show or a residency announcement. Arenas (United Center), rock halls (Thalia Hall, Salt Shed), listening rooms (Maurer Hall), concert halls (Symphony Center, The Auditorium). Jude's authority comes from having been in all of those rooms and knowing what each one does to sound.

**Corpus:** 3,536 words / 196 sentences. Avg 18 words per sentence — but bimodal: 17 sentences over 30 words, 26 sentences of 8 words or fewer. 36 contractions. Only 3 question marks in the whole corpus, all sign-offs.

---

## 1. The opener: a sensory claim about the room before the artist is named

Every single Jude piece opens on air, temperature, or silence — never on the artist. The artist arrives in sentence two or three.

Actual openers:

> "Standing on the banks of the Chicago River at the Salt Shed, you could feel the humidity of the crowd clashing with the cool April breeze coming off the water."

> "The Sunday night air in Chicago had a bite to it that felt appropriate for the arrival of Skullcrusher."

> "If you were anywhere near the West Side this past Monday or Tuesday, you felt the tectonic shift."

> "I've spent the last few days trying to shake the image of Lily Allen standing on the stage of The Auditorium Theatre this past Saturday."

**The signature construction — "There is a specific kind of ___":** four of seven pieces.

> "There is a specific kind of silence that descends on a room when the audience realizes they are in the presence of a once-in-a-generation technician."

> "There is a specific frequency of nostalgia that only Joe Hisaishi can tune into."

> "There is a specific kind of magic in a Chicago basement. It smells like stale Old Style, damp concrete, and the kind of reckless ambition that only exists when you are nineteen and convinced your guitar can dent the ceiling."

Use it once per piece. Never twice.

## 2. Sentence rhythm: long setup, then two short hammers

This is the core move. Build a clause-heavy sentence, then drop to five or six words.

> "This was not just another tour stop. This was Dave."

> "When Dave finally emerged, he did not start with a high-energy anthem. Instead, he sat at the piano. The room went dead silent."

> "The stage was sparse."

## 3. Negation-then-correction

Jude almost never states a thing positively on the first try. He denies the obvious reading, then supplies the real one. Semicolon or period, both appear.

> "It is not a bored silence; it is a breathless, wide-eyed sort of quiet."

> "She does not just play chords; she builds environments."

> "They didn't come from a surrealist woods or a David Lynch fever dream. They came from the grit of the city DIY scene."

> "TWICE didn't just play the United Center; they essentially annexed it."

> "the standing ovation felt less like a formality and more like a release of breath."

## 4. The crowd's body is the evidence

Jude does not say a show was good. He reports what the audience's nervous system did.

> "Hearing two thousand people shout-sing 'Fuck You' over the top of elegant violins"
> "the crowd—the 'ONCE' fandom—was vibrating"
> "a collective, audible sigh moved through the hall"
> "a hushed, confessional breath that forces three hundred people to stay absolutely still"
> "You can hear the radiator hum and the subtle creak of a floorboard three rows back."

Always cite the real capacity number when you have it: *"23,000 people scream-sing the chorus"*, *"three hundred people"*.

## 5. The venue gets its own paragraph

Non-negotiable. One paragraph, mid-piece, arguing the room and the artist were matched (or mismatched).

> "Maurer Hall is a listening room in the truest sense. It is a space designed for silence."

> "The Auditorium was the perfect venue for this. Its formal, seated grandeur grounded the messy, informal diatribes of the lyrics."

> "It has that grand, old world Chicago theater vibe, but when the right band hits the stage, it still feels as intimate as a house party."

## 6. Refuse the genre tag

Jude's recurring critical position: the label is smaller than the artist.

> "She is often labeled as a folk artist, but that tag feels increasingly reductive."
> "a group that has officially transcended the 'K-pop' label"
> "Lily Allen isn't just back; she's evolved into something much more interesting than a pop star. She's a storyteller now."
> "Cunningham has moved past the 'rising star' narrative. She is a foundational artist now."

## 7. Song-by-song, in bold, with a texture word each

Named tracks in quotes, each given one concrete sensory verdict — never "great" or "amazing":

> **"Tennis"**: A tense, coiling track that felt even tighter in the live setting.
> **"Fruityloop"**: The show ended on this track, a quiet, contained sigh that left the room in a heavy sort of silence.
> "The looping harmonies and echoing drumbeats created a sense of beautiful claustrophobia."
> "the interlocking guitar parts were so sharp they felt tactile"

## 8. The walk-out coda — mandatory

Every Jude piece ends by physically leaving the venue and finding the city changed.

> "We walked out into the West Town night feeling like we had just witnessed a landmark moment for the genre."

> "Leaving the theater and walking out into the Pilsen night, the music felt like it had re-calibrated the air."

> "Leaving Maurer Hall, the city streets felt a little softer and the yellow glow of the streetlights seemed a bit more intentional."

> "Leaving the Symphony Center and stepping back out onto Michigan Avenue, the city didn't look quite the same. The grit of the Loop felt softened."

## 9. Optional endings and appendices

- **A direct question to the reader** (2 of 7): *"Were you in the 'Candybong' glowing crowd for this one, or did you sit this one out?"* / *"Are you planning to attend the opening night, or do you prefer to wait...?"*
- **A bolded appendix block** after the body: `Setlist Notes:`, `Show Details:` (Venue / Dates / Vibe), or `The Essential Excerpt:` — a one-paragraph précis of the review.

## 10. Structure template

Four of seven use `<h3>` subheads; three run continuous. Subheads are two-to-five words, article-the heavy, and often signpost judgment rather than content:

`The Atmosphere` · `The Performance` · `The Takeaway` · `The Setlist: A Tale of Two Acts` · `Why It Worked (And Why It Didn't for Some)` · `The Breakdown: Hits, Remixed` · `The 10-Year Nostalgia Trip` · `Why It Matters` · `From Basements to the Main Stage` · `The Evolution of the Sound` · `Why Thalia Hall Matters`

Skeleton:

1. Air/temperature/silence + neighborhood geography (2–4 sentences)
2. What this show *was* structurally — tour concept, staging, act count
3. Opener credit, if there was one (one or two sentences, generous)
4. The artist's technique, argued against their genre label
5. Highlights: named tracks with texture verdicts
6. The venue paragraph
7. Why it matters *to Chicago* — are we a stopover or a litmus test
8. Walk-out coda
9. Optional reader question and/or appendix block

## Vocabulary

**Reach for:** grit · texture · tactile · architecture (of a song) · lineage · technician · craftsperson · juggernaut · tectonic · pacing · masterclass · unadulterated · spectral · hometown · litmus test · re-calibrated · breathless

**Chicago-specific furniture:** Old Style · the "L" · the West Side · the Loop · basement shows · stale beer and damp concrete · 90 percent humidity

**Rationing warning:** Jude over-uses *masterclass* (3×), *unadulterated* (2×), *specific kind of* (4×), and *less like ___ and more like ___* (3×). One of these per piece, maximum.

## Don't

- Don't open with the artist's name.
- Don't use star ratings, scores, or "verdict" language.
- Don't be mean about the crowd. Jude defends audiences — *"There's been some chatter online about her being 'stiff' or 'awkward' on this tour, but I didn't see it that way."*
- Don't quote press materials or interview the artist. Jude never has a quote from anyone on stage.
- Don't use exclamation points.
- Don't skip the walk-out.

## Worked opening (new, in voice)

> There is a specific kind of hum a room makes twenty minutes before a sold-out show, and Lincoln Hall had it bad on Thursday. The ceiling fans were losing. Somebody near the merch table was explaining the opener's discography to a stranger with the confidence of a man who had read one interview.
>
> This was not a nostalgia run. This was the first Chicago date of a record nobody has heard yet.
